§ 01

Background — a brief accounting.

Drop image here or click to upload
JPG · PNG · WEBP
Photographed at the faculty lounge, Diliman · 2024

I was born in San Fernando, La Union, the son of a public-school teacher and a municipal clerk. Most of what I know about teaching I learned from watching my mother grade papers at the kitchen table until midnight — and from the slow, patient criticism of mentors who took me seriously long before I had earned the right to be taken seriously.

My work sits at the intersection of teacher education, curriculum studies, and applied development research. I have spent the last decade teaching pre-service and in-service teachers, building research programs inside the College of Education, and advising public-sector partners on the messy, unglamorous work of curriculum reform. I am not interested in innovation as a slogan — only in the slow accumulation of small improvements that survive the year after they are introduced.

I currently hold a faculty appointment at the University of the Philippines Diliman, where I teach graduate courses in curriculum theory, foundations of education, and qualitative research methods. I direct a small research cell that studies teacher career trajectories in basic education, with field sites in Regions I and Cordillera. I write for a handful of peer-reviewed journals; I am not a frequent conference-goer; I prefer long documents to short ones.

Outside of work I read slowly, walk further than I should, and keep a small garden in Quezon City that has survived three summers and my inattention.

§ 03 — Philosophy
"

I do not
believe in teaching
— I believe in being taught.

— Working note, 2021

My teaching practice is built on a single, stubborn conviction: that the most important thing a teacher does is consent to be changed by the people in front of them. The classroom is not a delivery channel. It is a small, temporary public — a place where, for fifty minutes, we agree to think out loud together and accept the risk that we will leave the room different from how we entered.

I was trained in a tradition that took curriculum seriously as a moral document, not a technical one. The syllabus is a claim about what matters. The reading list is a claim about whose minds count. The assessment rubric is a claim about what kind of person we are trying to produce. None of these are neutral — and the moment a teacher forgets that, the classroom becomes a small bureaucracy of unexamined habits.

I am suspicious of the language of "excellence" when it is unattached to a particular good. I prefer the older word — virtue — because it carries the implication of practice, of failure, of patience. Excellence sounds like a trophy; virtue sounds like a habit you keep.

In practice, this means I write syllabi that are shorter than my colleagues', grade fewer assignments than the rubric expects, and ask my students to write more than I will ever return feedback on. I trust the surplus. I trust that the work they do that I never see is more important than the work I see.

Pillar 01

Patience over pace.

The curriculum does not have to be "covered." It has to be encountered. I would rather teach one essay carefully than five essays briskly.

Pillar 02

Honesty over polish.

I tell my students when a reading does not work, when an assignment is poorly designed, when I have changed my mind. They can tell the difference.

Pillar 03

Service over prestige.

The work a teacher does for an institution should be visible mainly to the institution. The work that matters most is rarely the work that gets cited.

Pillar 04

Repair over invention.

Most of what we need already exists and is broken. I prefer to fix what is there than to design what is new for the sake of newness.

§ 04

Values — what I will and will not do.

V — 01

Care is a method.

I do not treat care as a soft addition to instruction. The attention I pay to a student's name, to their previous writing, to the particular shape of their confusion — that is the instruction. Method without care is bureaucracy.

V — 02

Plain speech.

I avoid jargon when ordinary words will do. The reason most policy documents are unreadable is not that the ideas are hard but that the writers are protecting themselves from being held accountable for what they said.

V — 03

No invisible labour.

I name the people who did the work. Graduate students who co-authored are listed as co-authors. Research assistants who shaped the analysis are credited in the methods section. The first-author byline is not a trophy case.

V — 04

Decline work that cannot be done well.

I have learned to say no to invitations, commissions, and "quick" advisory roles where the timeline or budget does not permit the standard of work I would want attached to my name. A polite refusal is more honest than a thin deliverable.

V — 05

Field before desk.

When the fieldwork and the literature disagree, I trust the fieldwork first and re-read the literature. The reverse order is how a great deal of bad policy gets written.

V — 06

Refuse vanity.

I do not maintain a personal brand. I do not post photographs of the classrooms I teach in. I do not give inspirational keynote talks. The work is what I am willing to be remembered by, and nothing else.
