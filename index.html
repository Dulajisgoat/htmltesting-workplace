Teaching Philosophy
A classroom should ask more questions than it answers, and trust students to sit with that.

I plan backward from the questions I want students to still be asking a year later, not from the test at the end of the unit. Content is the vehicle; the habit of thinking rigorously is the actual destination.

In practice that means students talk more than I do, mistakes are treated as data rather than failure, and every unit ends with something made, argued, or built — not just recalled.

Values

What I hold to, on good days and bad ones.

Rigor with warmth
High expectations and genuine care aren't opposites — the second is what makes the first sustainable for students.
Honest feedback
Vague praise teaches nothing. I aim for feedback specific enough that a student knows exactly what to do next.
Equity in access
Differentiation isn't an accommodation bolted on afterward — it's built into how a unit is planned from the start.
Reflective practice
I keep a running log of what didn't work. The lessons that fail teach me more than the ones that land cleanly.
Collaboration over autonomy
The best units I've taught were co-planned. I default to sharing materials and asking colleagues to push back on them.
Steady presence
Students calibrate to the adult in the room. I try to be the same person on a hard Tuesday as I am on a good Friday.
